A four-stroke piston engine completes one thermodynamic/mechanical cycle in **four piston strokes and two crankshaft revolutions**:

1. **Intake** — piston moves down; intake valve opens and fresh charge enters.
2. **Compression** — piston moves up with valves closed, compressing the charge.
3. **Power** — combustion/high cylinder pressure drives the piston down.
4. **Exhaust** — piston moves up; exhaust valve opens and spent gases leave.

That means a given cylinder gets only **one power stroke every two crank revolutions**. In a four-cylinder engine, the cylinders are phased so power events are distributed through the cycle rather than all occurring together.

The **camshaft** in a conventional four-stroke engine rotates at **half crankshaft speed**, because the valves need one full sequence for every two crank rotations.

Gasoline and diesel engines share the four-stroke mechanical sequence but ignite differently: gasoline engines normally use a spark to ignite a premixed/port- or direct-injected charge; diesels compress air strongly enough that injected fuel autoignites in the hot compressed gas.

> **Takeaway:** The piston does not simply “explode downward.” Most of its motion is intake, compression, and exhaust machinery supporting the one pressure-producing power stroke.
