Life uses carbon compounds, but “organic” does not mean “made by an organism.” The deeper problem is how nonliving chemistry could repeatedly make useful products faster than dilution, decomposition and side reactions removed them.

**Big idea:** A source of ingredients, a catalyst and an energy supply solve different problems. Finding all three separately is not yet an explanation of how they worked together.

## What the spark experiment established

In 1953, Stanley Miller circulated gases and water through an apparatus with electrical discharges and recovered amino acids. The important result was chemical: biological building blocks could form without organisms. The experiment neither made life nor established the exact composition of Earth's early atmosphere.

Atmospheric composition matters because carbon in methane is chemically more reduced than carbon in carbon dioxide. “Reduced” concerns electron accounting, not the absence of oxygen atoms. Follow-up experiments varied gas mixtures and found major changes in yields and product diversity. A successful strongly reducing mixture cannot simply be relabelled “the early atmosphere.” Global conditions, transient impact-generated conditions and local volcanic conditions must be evaluated separately. [Miller (1953)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.117.3046.528) and [Miller and Schlesinger (1983)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11542461/) establish the experimental result and its dependence on starting chemistry.

## Energy is necessary; uncontrolled energy is destructive

A reaction may be thermodynamically favorable yet extremely slow because an activation barrier blocks it. A catalyst offers a faster route. It does not supply unlimited work or change the reaction's equilibrium merely by being present.

Conversely, an energy source is useful only when chemistry channels it. Ultraviolet light can drive bond rearrangements, but it can also destroy their products. Heat speeds competing reactions as well as the desired one. Hydrogen-rich fluids contacting more oxidized carbon compounds can provide chemical disequilibrium, but useful reduction still needs a viable mechanism.

A revealing example is [Patel and colleagues' 2015 reaction network](https://doi.org/10.1038/nchem.2202). Related chemistry involving hydrogen cyanide derivatives, hydrogen sulfide, ultraviolet light and copper photochemistry produced precursors relevant to nucleotides, amino acids and lipids. This challenges the assumption that each cellular subsystem required wholly unrelated starting chemistry. It did not produce an autonomous cell or show that every branch ran simultaneously in one undisturbed pond. Reagent availability, ordering and environmental compatibility remain part of the hypothesis.

## Why concentration is a mechanistic issue

Suppose a reaction needs an encounter between A and B, with rate per unit volume \(r=k[A][B]\). In an idealized evaporation step, reducing volume tenfold while retaining both solutes makes each concentration ten times larger. Then:

$$
r(after)=k(10[A])(10[B])=100r(before).
$$

The total number of reactions per second is rate per volume times volume, so it rises tenfold, not one hundredfold. This difference matters when comparing a small concentrated pool with a large dilute ocean. Real drying also changes salts, pH, precipitation and the rate constant; the arithmetic isolates only the encounter advantage.

Concentration can also help undesirable reactions. A good prebiotic setting must select and preserve useful intermediates, not merely make a dark, chemically complicated residue.

## Linking monomers is another hurdle

An amino acid is not a protein, and a nucleotide is not a genome. Polymer construction requires bonds to form with useful connectivity while competing with hydrolysis and unwanted branching. Modern cells solve these problems with activated substrates and catalysts; invoking those modern enzymes to explain their own origin would be circular.

Wet–dry cycles provide one experimentally explored route. [Forsythe and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1002/anie.201503792) combined amino acids with hydroxy acids. Cycling produced mixed ester–amide chains; exchange reactions and selective ester hydrolysis enriched peptide bonds over time. The interesting point is that a less stable kind of linkage can participate in a route toward a more stable one. This was controlled chemistry, not the spontaneous production of coded proteins.

An alternative source of driving force is continuous fluid flow. [Hudson and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2002659117) used a vent-inspired microfluidic system to couple hydrogen oxidation to carbon-dioxide reduction, producing formate across an iron–nickel sulfide interface with a pH gradient. The result supports a specific chemical step. It does not establish a complete self-sustaining metabolism.

## From a reaction to a lineage

A chemical product may accumulate because it is stable, supplied continuously or made autocatalytically. None alone guarantees open-ended evolution. To retain improvements, a system needs heritable differences that affect its continued reproduction. Sequence-bearing polymers offer one route: a change in sequence can alter function and be passed on.
