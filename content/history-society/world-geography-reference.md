A country becomes easier to remember when it has neighbors, an ocean, a river basin, and a place in a larger pattern. An isolated pair such as “Niger—Niamey” is fragile knowledge. Niger as a landlocked Sahel–Sahara country north of Nigeria, with Niamey on the Niger River in its southwest, has several routes back into memory.

**Big idea:** Learn the skeleton of the world before accumulating labels. Continents provide broad orientation; seas, mountain ranges, river basins and neighboring states make the orientation usable. Political history explains why the labels do not simply follow those physical features.

## What this reference includes

The six regional pages contain **195 unique baseline entries: 193 United Nations member states and the two non-member observer states, the Holy See and the State of Palestine**. This is a reproducible inclusion rule, not a verdict that only these entities can be states. The UN itself distinguishes membership from recognition, which other states grant or withhold.

**Reference checked: 11 October 2026.** The general capital-name baseline is the UN Statistics Division’s 2025 country profiles, supplemented by dated national-government and UN sources for exceptions and subsequent changes. In particular, the tables include Ciudad de la Paz’s 2026 designation in Equatorial Guinea and the UN’s 2026 adoption of Naoero for the country formerly called Nauru. Planned capital moves are not silently treated as completed moves.

Country names use familiar English short forms where useful: North Korea and South Korea correspond to the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea and Republic of Korea; Russia to the Russian Federation; Tanzania to the United Republic of Tanzania. The two Congos are always distinguished. Country membership survives ordinary changes of government or name; those are not new entries.

These are teaching regions, not a claim that the world has one uniquely correct set of continental boundaries. The UN M49 scheme is a useful statistical reference, but this arrangement deliberately groups Iran with West/Central Asia and uses a practical southern-African cluster. Russia appears once, in Europe, although most of its land is in Asia. Türkiye, Cyprus and the South Caucasus appear in West/Central Asia. Kazakhstan appears there too despite a small part lying west of the Ural River. Egypt appears in Africa despite Sinai being in Asia. Moving any of these between regional shelves would not change the baseline count.

Antarctica has no sovereign-state entry. Nor do dependent territories become additional baseline states simply because they are geographically far from their administering country. [Sovereignty, capitals and territories](/entry/sovereignty-capitals-territories) explains the important cases outside the baseline, including Taiwan, Kosovo, Somaliland, Western Sahara, the Cook Islands and Niue.

## Four passes that turn a list into geography

1. **Outline:** On a published world map, find oceans, broad landmasses, the Mediterranean, Caribbean, Red Sea and Persian Gulf. Use the [UN Geospatial world map](https://www.un.org/geospatial/content/map-world-1) as a labeled reference; read its boundary notes and edition date.
2. **Neighborhood:** Work through one subregion at a time. Trace a coast or a chain of neighbors. Ask which countries interrupt a simple route: the Gambia almost surrounded by Senegal, with an Atlantic outlet, Lesotho within South Africa, or the two parts of Malaysia separated by sea.
3. **Capital:** Attach the capital to a physical or political role: river junction, port, inland planned center, federal compromise, or divided institutional seat. Do not assume the capital is the largest city.
4. **Explanation:** Follow a few history links. Why are two countries on Hispaniola? Why do Central Asia’s international borders cross closely connected valleys? Why does French Guiana not appear as an independent country? Labels become memorable when they explain something.

The regional tables give **selected orientation anchors**, not exhaustive border inventories. “Between” describes broad location; it does not imply every named state is an immediate neighbor or that all intermediate territory is undisputed. Seas named in a location cue identify bodies of water, not a position on competing maritime claims.

## A worked comparison: landlocked is not the same as isolated

Compare **Switzerland**, **Niger**, and **Paraguay**. None has an ocean coast. Yet their access problems differ. Switzerland sits amid dense European transport networks and has a navigable Rhine connection to North Sea ports. Niger’s ocean access depends on long overland corridors through other West African states. Paraguay can reach the Atlantic through the Paraguay–Paraná river system and neighboring countries.

The useful deduction is not “landlocked countries are poor” or “rivers guarantee prosperity.” Access depends on infrastructure, river navigability, border procedures, security and relations with transit states. Physical geography creates costs and opportunities; institutions and history shape how those are used. The map is a starting constraint, not a complete explanation of outcomes.

## What a capital label does and does not mean

“Capital” can identify a constitutional designation, the main executive seat, a legislature, or a historically customary federal center. Sometimes these coincide; sometimes they do not. The Hague does not cease to house Dutch government because Amsterdam is the capital. Pretoria does not contain every institution of South Africa. An announced new capital does not prove that all ministries have moved.

The regional exception notes preserve these distinctions at the point of use. They also separate a claimed capital from international agreement about sovereignty. A table that confidently erases such distinctions teaches the wrong fact even when its spelling is correct.
