Europe in 1815 was not organized into the familiar collection of nation-states. German-speaking and Italian-speaking populations lived across multiple states, while large dynastic empires governed many languages. The defeat of Napoleon created a choice about how to reconstruct order, not an opportunity to restore an unchanged past.

**Big idea:** The Congress of Vienna tried to stabilize relations between rulers. Nationalism increasingly asked whether those rulers and their territories represented a people. These were different tests of legitimacy.

## What Napoleon changed

The French Revolution and Napoleonic conquests spread legal and administrative changes while also imposing occupation, extraction and military recruitment. Responses therefore mixed attraction to reform with resistance to French domination. National mobilization could borrow revolutionary ideas while opposing the army that carried them.

The Holy Roman Empire ended in 1806. Its disappearance and the consolidation of German political units could not simply be reversed by announcing a return to tradition. The [German Historical Museum's chronology](https://www.dhm.de/archiv/ausstellungen/staendige-ausstellung/1789-1871/index.html) places this transformation in the longer route to 1871.

## Vienna was a negotiated balance

The congress met in 1814–15, with Britain, Austria, Russia and Prussia dominant and defeated France also participating. The Final Act was signed on 9 June 1815, before Waterloo on 18 June. That sequence matters: the settlement was already being negotiated when Napoleon returned from exile.

The negotiators balanced restoration of dynasties, strategic compensation and the prevention of renewed French predominance. Prussia gained important western lands on the Rhine; Russia obtained much of the former Duchy of Warsaw; Austria strengthened its Italian position. Disputes over Poland and Saxony showed that the victors did not share one harmonious design. [Fondation Napoléon account](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/did-the-congress-of-vienna-undo-everything-napoleon-had-achieved/).

The German Confederation linked sovereign states rather than creating a unified German nation-state. Reintegration of France and consultation among major powers helped sustain a European balance, but this was an unequal club, not universal collective security. [German Historical Institute source presentation](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/from-vormaerz-to-prussian-dominance-1815-1866/the-congress-of-vienna-under-the-leadership-of-clemens-prince-von-metternich-1814-15).

## What “a century of peace” gets wrong

Europe avoided another conflict on the scale of the Napoleonic Wars for much of the nineteenth century. That is not the same as a century without war. The Crimean War, wars of Italian and German unification, rebellions and colonial wars were major conflicts. A settlement can restrain certain kinds of war while legitimizing coercion elsewhere.

The distinction also changes whose perspective counts. A British diplomat might judge the balance successful while a Polish nationalist rejected the denial of an independent Polish state. An Italian liberal could oppose Austrian influence without agreeing with every rival Italian movement about the state that should replace it.

## Why language did not dictate the new map

Nationalism offers a powerful claim: political authority should belong to a people. It does not supply a universally agreed algorithm for deciding where that people begins and ends. Language, historical memory, religion, territory and political aspiration can point in different directions.

German unification illustrates the problem. The German Empire formed in 1871 under Prussian leadership after wars against Denmark, Austria and France. Austria was excluded. The ceremonial proclamation at Versailles dramatized victory over France, but constitutional agreements and bargaining among German governments also mattered. [German Historical Museum account](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/kapitel/kaiserreich/reich/reichsgruendung).

A language map alone cannot explain this outcome. It cannot tell us why German-speaking Austria remained outside, why Prussia led, or why minority populations lived within the empire. Unification was contingent state-building with nationalist legitimacy, not geography discovering its inevitable shape.

Italian unification likewise combined popular mobilization, diplomacy, warfare and the expansion of a particular monarchy. Remember the comparison rather than a list of rulers: national movements can create larger states, while other national movements seek separation from existing ones.

## Britain was also made, not given

The United Kingdom should not be treated as an ancient fixed unit while other countries are described as constructed. In 1707, parliamentary union joined England and Scotland into Great Britain; the monarchies had already shared a ruler since 1603. The union preserved important Scottish institutional differences rather than dissolving all distinctions. [Articles of Union](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/legislativescrutiny/act-of-union-1707/overview/the-articles-constitution-and-trade/).

Union with Ireland took effect in 1801. Partition and the creation of the Irish Free State in 1922 later left Northern Ireland within the UK. The [1920 Act and its aftermath](https://lordslibrary.parliament.uk/government-of-ireland-act-1920-what-system-did-it-create/) show again why legal dates, effective government and competing national claims must be separated.

## A compact causal timeline

| Date | Change | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1789–1815 | Revolution and Napoleonic warfare | Reform, imperial domination and resistance reshape legitimacy |
| 1806 | Holy Roman Empire ends | The old German framework cannot simply be restored |
| 1814–15 | Vienna settlement | Great-power balance and dynastic reconstruction |
| 1848 | Revolutions across much of Europe | Liberal and national demands confront existing regimes |
| 1861–71 | Key phases of Italian and German unification | Larger states transform the continental balance |
| 1921–22 | Irish partition and Free State | National state-building also divides an existing union |

Vienna and nationalism did not form a simple contest between an obsolete past and a predestined future. Their interaction reveals two recurring questions: **Can a settlement keep powerful states from destroying one another? Can the people it governs regard it as legitimate?** Solving one does not automatically solve the other.
