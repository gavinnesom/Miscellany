The most useful organizing features here are **the Turkish Straits, Caucasus, eastern Mediterranean, Tigris–Euphrates basin, Arabian Peninsula, Iranian plateau and landlocked Central Asian interior**. “Middle East” is a variable historical and political label; it is not a continent with universally fixed edges.

**Big idea:** A small coastal position can matter more for access than a large land area. Türkiye sits across the passage from Black Sea to Mediterranean; Oman’s Musandam Peninsula overlooks the Strait of Hormuz; Djibouti and Yemen face the Bab el-Mandeb corridor linking the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden.

## Connect the regions rather than memorizing separate blocks

- **Black Sea to Caspian:** Georgia has a Black Sea coast; Azerbaijan has a Caspian coast; Armenia lies inland between its neighbors. The Caucasus mountains do not make every political boundary self-evident.
- **Mediterranean to Mesopotamia:** Lebanon and Israel face the Mediterranean; Jordan lies inland except for its short Gulf of Aqaba coast. Iraq centers on the Tigris–Euphrates river system and has only a narrow Gulf outlet. Syria connects the Mediterranean side to the interior.
- **Around Arabia:** Saudi Arabia occupies most of the peninsula. Follow the Gulf coast from Kuwait through Saudi Arabia, Bahrain and Qatar toward the UAE. Oman turns toward the Arabian Sea; Yemen occupies the southwest. Bahrain is an island state; Qatar is a peninsula.
- **Across the inland belt:** Iran connects the Caspian and Persian Gulf regions. North and northeast lie the five post-Soviet Central Asian states. Kazakhstan is the large northern steppe state; Turkmenistan occupies the southeastern Caspian shore; Uzbekistan sits centrally; Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan are mountainous eastern neighbors.

The Caspian is an enclosed inland water body: a Caspian coast is not direct access to the world ocean. Uzbekistan is **doubly landlocked**: every neighboring state is also landlocked in the ocean-access sense. This makes transport corridors and transit agreements part of ordinary economic geography.

## Capital and status notes

- **W1 — Cyprus:** Nicosia is the capital of the Republic of Cyprus. The island has divided administration, a UN buffer zone and UK sovereign base areas. The self-declared Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus is recognized only by Türkiye; northern Nicosia is its administrative center. The baseline row is not a claim that the Republic exercises control everywhere. [UN report on the northern administration](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4086509/files/A_HRC_60_35_Add.2-EN.pdf)
- **W2 — South Caucasus:** Abkhazia and South Ossetia have separate de facto authorities and limited recognition, while UN usage places them within Georgia. Nagorno-Karabakh should not be copied from an old atlas as an enduring separate state; Azerbaijan regained control in 2023. These notes do not attempt a live territorial-control map. See the [exceptions guide](/entry/sovereignty-capitals-territories).
- **W3 — Israel/Palestine:** Israel designates Jerusalem as its capital and locates central state institutions there; international recognition of its sovereignty over the city is contested. Palestinians claim East Jerusalem as their capital; Ramallah is an administrative center in the West Bank. The UN reference lists Jerusalem and East Jerusalem with qualifications. The West Bank, including East Jerusalem, and Gaza are the occupied Palestinian territory in UN usage; a capital cell does not resolve borders, occupation or sovereignty. [UN profiles: Israel](https://data.un.org/legacy/en/iso/il.html), [Palestine](https://data.un.org/legacy/en/iso/ps.html)
- **W4 — Yemen:** Sana’a is the capital in the UN reference. The internationally recognized government has used Aden as a temporary capital while Sana’a has been under Houthi control. This distinction is essential; neither city label is a reliable real-time summary of the wider war. [UN Special Envoy, Aden dialogue report](https://osesgy.unmissions.org/en/node/29188)

## Worked example: why a border can create a transport problem

Azerbaijan’s Nakhchivan exclave does not adjoin the country’s main territory. Armenia lies between them; Nakhchivan also borders Iran and a short stretch of Türkiye. A line on a map therefore raises several distinct questions: Can people transit another state? Under whose jurisdiction? With what customs and security procedures? A proposed transport corridor does not automatically imply transfer of sovereignty over the land it crosses.

The same distinction scales up. Landlocked Central Asian states can gain access through railways and ports in neighboring states, but that access depends on both infrastructure and political agreements. Geography supplies the route options; it does not predetermine which alliance a state must join.
