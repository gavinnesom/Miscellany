The First World War destroyed dynastic orders across Europe and the Middle East. Yet the peace did not simply replace four empires with a tidy set of national homes. War, revolution, competing claims and unequal power created new states with new minorities, while overseas imperial rule continued in altered forms.

**Main message:** Ending fighting, allocating territory, protecting populations and building a workable security order are separate tasks. A peace agreement can address one while failing at another.

## From a Balkan crisis to world war

The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 triggered a crisis between Austria-Hungary and Serbia. It did not contain an automatic world war within it. Austro-Hungarian leaders chose coercion and war against Serbia; German support increased their room to act. Russian mobilization, German war planning and declarations, and French commitments widened the crisis. Germany's invasion of Belgium helped bring Britain into the war.

The common “alliance dominoes” model hides decision-making. Italy, formally associated with Germany and Austria-Hungary before the war, did not join them in 1914 and entered on the other side in 1915. Governments interpreted obligations through strategic calculations. Historians dispute the relative weight of responsibility, preventive-war thinking and miscalculation; that debate is not evidence that no choices can be identified.

Empires made the war global from its early stages. Soldiers, workers, shipping and resources came from far beyond Europe, while campaigns extended into Africa, the Middle East and Asia. Colonial participants were not a decorative addition to a European war. Their labor and military service were part of how it could be fought. [National Army Museum overview](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/first-world-war).

## Why 1917 changed the conflict

Revolution undermined Russia's participation, eventually leading the Bolshevik government to make peace at Brest-Litovsk in March 1918. The United States entered the war against Germany in April 1917. Renewed unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram helped move American policy from neutrality to belligerency. Wilson's public case also framed participation in terms of political principles. His [address to Congress](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/address-to-congress-declaration-of-war-against-germany) is evidence of the case he made, not a complete account of every American motive.

Industrial mobilization, blockade, food shortages, battlefield losses and political legitimacy interacted. Empires did not fall simply because one battle changed a map. Their capacity to keep populations supplied, armies obedient and governments credible broke down unevenly.

## The armistice was not the whole peace

The 11 November 1918 armistice ended fighting with Germany on the Western Front. Civil wars, border wars and other violence continued. The main settlements were separate treaties, not one universal Treaty of Versailles:

| Settlement | Defeated power concerned | A central consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Versailles, 1919 | Germany | Territorial losses, disarmament obligations, reparations and League framework |
| Saint-Germain, 1919 | Austria | Recognition of a drastically reduced Austrian state and Habsburg breakup |
| Neuilly, 1919 | Bulgaria | Territorial losses and other obligations |
| Trianon, 1920 | Hungary | Major territorial losses following imperial disintegration |
| Sèvres, 1920; superseded by Lausanne, 1923 | Ottoman state, then Turkey | A signed treaty that never entered into force was superseded after war and renegotiation |

The [USHMM overview](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/world-war-i-treaties-and-reparations?series=20) explains the treaty system and German grievances. The scholarly [Paris Peace Conference overview](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/article/the_paris_peace_conference_and_its_consequences/2014-10-08) extends the chronology through 1923.

## Self-determination had no simple map algorithm

Wilson's [Fourteen Points](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/president-woodrow-wilsons-14-points) proposed an international settlement including a restored Poland and arrangements for peoples formerly under imperial rule. But the rhetoric did not mean every community, everywhere, would receive a sovereign state.

Consider Poland. Re-establishing a state divided among empires answered a national claim, but its borders also raised questions about ports, railways, military security and populations speaking different languages. Access to the Baltic and the status of Danzig were not reducible to coloring each village by ethnicity. Czechoslovakia and the new South Slav kingdom similarly contained major internal differences.

This is a structural problem: a boundary can maximize one group's national claim while creating another group's minority status. Plebiscites can help in particular districts but do not determine which districts vote, who qualifies, or how economic networks should be divided. Minority protections and citizenship therefore matter even after a supposedly national settlement.

## The colonial exception

Former German colonies and Ottoman territories were assigned to a League mandate system. Article 22 classified populations and territories in an explicitly hierarchical framework, with different degrees of tutelage. Britain, France and other victorious powers retained major governing roles. [League Covenant text](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/imt/parti.asp).

The mandate system changed the legal language and introduced international supervision; it did not implement universal immediate self-rule. Anticolonial movements could use the gap between proclaimed principles and actual governance to challenge the settlement. The defeated empires lost territory while victorious empires often gained responsibilities and leverage.

## Did Versailles cause the next war?

The settlements contributed grievances and strategic vulnerabilities, but “Versailles made Hitler inevitable” is not a causal explanation. It omits the Great Depression, political choices, Nazi ideology, rearmament, aggression and the failures of collective security. Nor were all complaints equivalent: acknowledging German grievances does not justify conquest or genocide.

The better comparison with [Vienna](/entry/vienna-nationalism-unification) asks about enforceability, inclusion, economic recovery and legitimacy. Neither settlement succeeded merely by finding a clever line on a map. The institutions and political choices sustaining the line were part of the settlement itself.
