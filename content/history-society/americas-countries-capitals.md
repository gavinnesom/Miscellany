The Americas have a long western mountain system, broad eastern lowlands, a narrow Central American land connection, and an island region around the Caribbean. Political and linguistic labels cut across this physical structure: **Latin America is not synonymous with South America**, and the Caribbean is not composed entirely of islands or independent states.

**Big idea:** Coastlines and watersheds orient the map; conquest, settlement, slavery, independence and state-building explain many of its political and linguistic divisions. Neither layer replaces the other.

## A north-to-south route

- **Northern America:** Canada spans three ocean frontages. The contiguous United States lies south of it, but Alaska is separated from those states by Canada and Hawaii is in the Pacific. Mexico lies partly in the same continental landmass and is culturally/historically often grouped with Latin America.
- **Central American isthmus:** From Mexico go through Guatemala, then Honduras/Nicaragua/Costa Rica to Panama. Belize sits on the Caribbean side beside Guatemala; El Salvador sits on the Pacific side. A simple chain omits those important side branches.
- **Caribbean arcs:** Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica and Puerto Rico belong to the Greater Antilles. Smaller islands curve south and east toward Trinidad near Venezuela. The Bahamas lie north of the Caribbean Sea in the Atlantic, while remaining part of the wider Caribbean region in many political/cultural classifications.
- **South America:** The Andes run along the western margin; the Amazon basin occupies much of the north-central interior; the Paraná–Paraguay–Río de la Plata system drains much of the southeast. Brazil touches every South American sovereign state except Chile and Ecuador.

The tables include 35 UN member states. Puerto Rico and French Guiana are not missing countries: their statuses differ from those of neighboring independent states. Greenland belongs in North American physical geography but has a constitutional connection to Denmark.

## Capital and status notes

- **M1 — Guyana/Venezuela:** Venezuela claims the Essequibo region administered by Guyana. The countries’ location cues do not endorse that claim or erase the dispute. The International Court of Justice case concerns the 1899 arbitral award and related issues; provisional measures are not a final judgment on the merits. [ICJ case record](https://www.icj-cij.org/case/171)
- **M2 — Bolivia:** Sucre is the constitutional capital; La Paz is the seat of government, including executive and legislative institutions. A one-city answer is incomplete when the question does not specify which role. [UN profile and footnote](https://data.un.org/legacy/en/iso/bo.html)

## Similar-looking names conceal different histories

**Dominica** is a small island in the Lesser Antilles with Roseau as capital. The **Dominican Republic** occupies the eastern part of Hispaniola and has Santo Domingo as capital. **Haiti** occupies Hispaniola’s western part. These are three states on two islands, not three alternative names for one place.

Likewise, **Guyana** and **Suriname** are independent mainland South American states. **French Guiana**, to Suriname’s east, is part of France. A physical map of the Guiana region therefore crosses two independent states and an integral part of France rather than describing a single sovereign unit. The [territories guide](/entry/sovereignty-capitals-territories) separates geography from statehood.

## Worked example: two landlocked states with river access

Bolivia and Paraguay are South America’s two landlocked sovereign states. Both lack an ocean coast, but do not imagine the same landscape. Bolivia includes high Andean terrain and lowland basins; Paraguay sits primarily in the inland river system east of the Andes. Navigable waterways and transit arrangements can connect inland production to Atlantic ports, although that does not remove dependence on neighboring jurisdictions.

The distinction helps prevent geographical determinism. “No coast” is a meaningful constraint, but it does not specify transport cost, political stability, wealth or diplomatic alignment without additional evidence.

## Capitals can be deliberate political choices

Washington, Ottawa and Brasília illustrate why a capital need not be the largest commercial city. The task of locating government can involve regional bargaining, security, existing transport networks or a deliberate attempt to shift development inland. Learning the city’s role makes it less tempting to substitute New York, Toronto or Rio de Janeiro by familiarity alone.
