The United States began by rejecting British imperial authority, later expanded across a continent, fought a civil war over a slaveholding secession, and eventually acquired overseas colonies. Those are connected developments, not interchangeable expressions of a single national destiny.

**Big idea:** Independence answers who governs a territory from outside. Emancipation, citizenship, and the distribution of land answer different questions inside it.

## Independence was an international war

Britain's victory in the Seven Years' War left it with enlarged commitments and disputes over how its North American colonies should help pay for imperial defense. Colonists' resistance to taxation and parliamentary authority escalated into war in 1775 and a declaration of independence in 1776. Not all colonists supported the revolution; Loyalists, enslaved people and Indigenous nations made choices under different pressures.

The American military struggle became part of a wider contest involving France, Spain and the Netherlands. French participation was crucial, but supporting the Americans did not make the French monarchy a champion of universal republicanism. Weakening Britain was also a strategic objective.

The [1783 Treaty of Paris](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/treaty-of-paris-0) recognized US independence and defined a large claimed territory extending to the Mississippi. A British–American agreement did not mean Indigenous peoples had consented to surrender their lands. Diplomatic recognition and actual possession were separate problems.

## A comparison that exposes the limits of a slogan

In the French colony of Saint-Domingue, enslaved people launched the revolution that culminated in Haitian independence in 1804. Their struggle defeated efforts to reimpose colonial domination and slavery. Haiti's subsequent isolation and pressure from foreign powers show that military independence did not guarantee equal treatment in the international system. [Library of Congress profile](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/frd/copr/Haiti.pdf).

The comparison with the United States is instructive. American revolutionary language could be used against slavery, but the new republic retained it. In Haiti, emancipation was central to the revolutionary state itself. Neither story is adequately described by saying that an existing “people” simply acquired its own flag.

Spanish American independence movements and Brazil's separation from Portugal followed their own struggles and constitutional paths. Napoleon's intervention in Iberia helped open a crisis of royal legitimacy, but local political coalitions and wars determined what followed. Independence did not produce one unified Spanish-speaking state, and Brazil became an independent monarchy before becoming a republic. The [Library of Congress's comparative symposium](https://loc.gov/loc/lcib/11012/freedom.html) usefully frames the Americas as interacting histories rather than copies of the US Revolution.

## Expansion transformed the slavery question

The United States' westward growth involved purchases of imperial claims, settler movement, treaties, forced removal and war. The Louisiana Purchase of 1803 transferred French claims; it did not amount to a purchase of Indigenous consent. The US–Mexican War ended with the [Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in 1848](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/treaty-of-guadalupe-hidalgo), which transferred a vast northern part of Mexico and addressed residents' nationality and property rights.

The geographic payoff is concrete: much of the American Southwest did not become Spanish-speaking because recent migrants brought an otherwise foreign language to it. Spanish-speaking communities were already there when sovereignty changed.

New territories also reopened the question of slavery's extension. Every prospective state could change representation and the balance between free and slave states. The argument was therefore about the future power of a national system, not merely whether one plantation would continue operating. Compromises could postpone the issue without removing the mechanism generating it.

## What caused secession, and what changed during the war?

Slavery was central to the secession crisis of 1860–61. Secession declarations and Confederate leaders' statements are unusually direct evidence. “States' rights” is incomplete unless one asks which rights and whose interests: slaveholders also sought federal protection and enforcement of slave property claims. Lincoln's election on a platform opposing slavery's territorial extension convinced secessionists that their long-term position was threatened. [National Park Service evidence overview](https://www.nps.gov/liho/learn/historyculture/slavery-cause-civil-war.htm).

The Union initially fought to preserve the country, while the war increasingly became a struggle over emancipation as well. Enslaved people who escaped to Union lines undermined slavery themselves; Black military service helped turn freedom into a claim on citizenship. Government policy and action from below interacted.

The Emancipation Proclamation was a wartime measure with a defined territorial scope. The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in 1865, abolished slavery and involuntary servitude nationwide except as punishment for a crime after conviction. The exception matters; so does the difference between constitutional abolition and secure equality in practice. [Text and context](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/13th-amendment).

Reconstruction then contested citizenship, voting, labor and federal protection. Constitutional change did not prevent organized violence and the later construction of segregation and disenfranchisement. Winning the war settled secession militarily; it did not settle who would enjoy equal freedom.

## The chronology without a destiny story

| Date | Transformation | Important distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1775–83 | American war for independence | Domestic revolution and international power struggle |
| 1791–1804 | Haitian Revolution | Emancipation and independence reinforce each other |
| Early nineteenth century | Independence across much of Latin America | Shared imperial predecessors do not imply one successor state |
| 1848 | US–Mexico peace settlement | A border moves across inhabited societies |
| 1861–65 | American Civil War | Slavery-centered secession; Union aims and emancipation develop during war |
| 1865 onward | Abolition and Reconstruction | Legal freedom, citizenship and effective rights are not the same |
| 1898–early 1900s | US overseas expansion | An ex-colony can itself become an imperial power |

After the Spanish–American War, US power extended through Caribbean and Pacific possessions, followed by war against Filipino independence forces. This complicates any simple contrast between an imperial Europe and an automatically anti-imperial United States. [Office of the Historian overview](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1899-1913/foreword).

The durable question is therefore: **whose sovereignty expanded, whose autonomy contracted, and which institutions survived the change?**
