Immanuel Kant (**1724–1804**) tries to explain how objective knowledge is possible after Hume undermined the idea that necessities such as causation are simply read from experience.

> **Big idea:** The mind is not a blank camera pointed at a fully pre-structured world. Human experience has a form partly contributed by the cognitive conditions under which anything can appear to us as an object at all.

Kant calls **space and time** forms of intuition and describes categories such as **causality** as conditions organizing experience. That is his “Copernican” shift: instead of assuming cognition must conform passively to objects as they are in themselves, ask how objects *as experienced* conform to the structure of cognition.

This produces the famous distinction between:

- **phenomena** — things as they appear within the conditions of possible human experience;
- **things in themselves (noumena)** — reality considered independently of those conditions, which Kant argues we cannot know in the same way.

In ethics, Kant grounds morality in **autonomy** and rational consistency. One formulation of the categorical imperative says to act only on a maxim you could will as a universal law; another says to treat humanity never merely as a means but also as an end.

A misconception: Kant is not saying “reality is just made up by your mind.” He is trying to distinguish the contribution of the knowing subject from whatever exists independently of us.
