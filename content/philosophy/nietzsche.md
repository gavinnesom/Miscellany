Friedrich Nietzsche (**1844–1900**) often asks a different question from “Is this moral value true?” He asks **where did this value come from, what kind of life produced it, and what does it do to the people who live by it?** This historical/psychological mode is his **genealogical** critique.

> **Big idea:** Values have histories. Treating inherited morality as timeless can hide the psychological and social forces that created it.

The **“death of God”** is not a triumphant one-line proof of atheism. It is a cultural diagnosis: European moral and intellectual life had increasingly undermined the Christian-metaphysical framework that supplied ultimate authority, while continuing to rely on many of its inherited values. The resulting danger is **nihilism** — loss of convincing grounds for value and meaning.

Nietzsche contrasts forms of morality, famously **master** and **slave morality**, to explore how power, resentment (*ressentiment*), strength, vulnerability, and social reversal shape moral vocabularies. These are provocative genealogical models, not neutral anthropology.

His **Übermensch** is often mangled into a racial or comic-book “superman.” In context it is connected to overcoming inherited values and creating/affirming new forms of life, not a Nazi racial ideal. Nietzsche himself died before Nazism; later appropriation was aided by selective editing and political use of his work.
