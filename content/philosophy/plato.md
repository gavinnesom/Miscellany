Plato (c. **428/427–348/347 BCE**) writes philosophy mainly as dialogues rather than textbooks. Socrates is often the central character, which makes Plato both a source for Socrates and a philosopher whose own views develop through dramatic argument.

> **Big idea:** Plato repeatedly distinguishes **appearance from intelligible structure**: what we perceive and conventionally believe may depend on more stable realities or standards that reason tries to grasp.

His famous **Forms** are not simply perfect physical copies floating somewhere else. In dialogues such as the *Republic*, Forms provide stable objects of knowledge — Beauty itself, Justice itself, and ultimately the Good — against which changing particulars can be understood. Scholars disagree about exactly how uniformly this theory should be attributed across Plato's works.

The **Allegory of the Cave** dramatizes the epistemic problem: prisoners mistake shadows for reality because those shadows are all they have learned to interpret. Education is therefore not just adding facts; it can involve turning the mind toward a different level of explanation.

In the *Republic*, Plato links psychology and politics: reason, spirit, and appetite within a person are mirrored in his idealized classes of the city. That political construction has been enormously influential and also deeply criticized for authoritarian features.

A misconception worth avoiding: “Platonic” does not simply mean believing every object has one obvious perfect blueprint. Plato's dialogues often explore problems rather than handing us a tidy doctrine manual.
