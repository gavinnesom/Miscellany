Aristotle (**384–322 BCE**) was Plato's student, but his surviving work ranges far beyond a reaction against Plato: logic, ethics, politics, metaphysics, rhetoric, poetry, psychology, and detailed biological investigation.

> **Big idea:** To understand a thing, Aristotle often asks for several kinds of explanation at once rather than searching for one single cause.

His famous **four causes** are:

- **material cause** — what it is made from;
- **formal cause** — the organization/form that makes it this kind of thing;
- **efficient cause** — what brings it about or changes it;
- **final cause** — the end/function toward which it is directed.

Modern science generally does not treat nature as universally goal-directed in Aristotle's sense, but functional explanation remains familiar in biology and engineering when used carefully.

In ethics, the central idea is **eudaimonia** — human flourishing, not merely a passing feeling of happiness. Character is formed through habituation: we become courageous, generous, or just partly by repeatedly practicing the relevant kinds of action. Virtue often involves finding the appropriate mean relative to circumstances, guided by **practical wisdom (phronesis)**.

“The golden mean” is frequently caricatured as “always choose the moderate middle.” Aristotle's point is not arithmetic compromise: courage is a mean between cowardice and rashness, but the right action depends on situation, motive, person, and judgment.
